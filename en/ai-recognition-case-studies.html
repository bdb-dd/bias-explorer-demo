AI Recognition Case Studies

Three complete hypothetical cases for a new experiment: each one follows a recognisable decision-maker who tries to use AI to identify high achievers, makes plausible mistakes with data and model usage, and ends up rewarding visibility or structural advantage rather than merit. The point is not that the persona is careless. The point is that the workflow feels disciplined right up until the moment it quietly stops being fair.

Shared Setup
Legitimate goal, messy evidence, polished AI output Each case keeps the same teaching structure so the failures are comparable across domains.
Teaching Focus
Data bias, proxy metrics, and over-trust in synthesis These cases work best when readers can see how reasonable intent still produces a distorted decision.

Case 01 · Visibility Bias

The Engineering Director Who Wanted to Reward the Real Drivers of a Launch

Marta is an engineering director at a mid-sized software company. Her team just shipped a difficult platform migration on time, with only minor launch-day issues. She wants to recognize the people who made the biggest difference, but she does not want the decision to turn into a popularity contest or default to whoever speaks most in meetings.

Objective

Identify who most deserves recognition for the migration launch.

Available Data

  • GitHub pull requests: visible code changes, not overall impact.
  • Jira tickets: closure volume, not strategic difficulty.
  • Slack launch messages: public activity, not quiet problem prevention.
  • Retrospective notes: selective memory written after the fact.
  • Peer feedback: useful context, but unevenly detailed.

Hidden Reality

A staff engineer removed a major architectural risk six weeks earlier, and an engineering manager resolved cross-team dependency conflicts. Both contributions were decisive but lightly documented.

Flawed Prompt

Review these launch artifacts and identify the three individuals who contributed most materially to the success of the migration. Rank them and explain your reasoning with evidence from the repository, project tracker, launch channel, and retrospective.

What happened

The model returns a polished ranking. It highlights an engineer with an unusually high commit count, another who closed a large number of tickets in the final sprint, and a third who appears constantly in the launch Slack thread answering questions. Marta likes the output because it looks evidence-based rather than intuitive. In practice, the model is ranking what was easiest to count and easiest to quote.

Where the reasoning breaks

Data Mistake

Visible engineering artifacts are treated as a reasonably complete record of contribution.

AI-use Mistake

The model is asked to infer true impact from proxies like commit volume, ticket counts, and message visibility.

Process Mistake

No calibrated review with other leaders happens before recognition is finalized.

Why the outcome is wrong

The ranking rewards people whose work leaves strong traces in systems and public channels. Quiet risk reduction, coordination, mentorship, and conflict resolution start to look secondary even when they were decisive. The harm is not only that the wrong people are recognized. The organization also learns that visible output is what counts.

What a better version would do

Marta could still use AI, but only to summarize evidence by contribution type rather than rank people outright. A stronger process would separate implementation, risk reduction, coordination, and incident response; question whether some crucial work is under-recorded; and require a manager review that explicitly asks whose impact is likely invisible in the available data.

Case 02 · Institutional Record Bias

The Principal Who Tried to Make an Excellence Award More Holistic

Dr. Alvarez is a high school principal choosing the recipient of the school's annual excellence award. She does not want to reward grades alone. She wants to consider leadership, resilience, service, and character, and believes AI might help her run a more balanced review than a rushed manual comparison.

Objective

Choose the student who most deserves the annual excellence award.

Available Data

  • GPA and course rigor: meaningful, but narrow.
  • Attendance: may reflect caregiving or paid work.
  • Discipline records: vulnerable to unequal enforcement.
  • Teacher comments: variable in tone and detail.
  • Extracurriculars and service: easier to document for students with more time and resources.

Hidden Reality

A finalist with comparable academic strength works part-time, helps care for younger siblings, and has thinner formal documentation of service and leadership.

Flawed Prompt

Compare these finalists holistically and recommend the student who best represents overall excellence, including academics, leadership, service, resilience, and character. Explain the recommendation clearly.

What happened

The model recommends a highly visible student with strong grades, glowing comments, multiple leadership roles, no disciplinary history, and well-documented volunteer work. The answer reads as careful and balanced, which reassures Dr. Alvarez. But the review is only as fair as the underlying records, and the records were never neutral to begin with.

Where the reasoning breaks

Data Mistake

The combined student record is treated as a fair portrait of merit rather than a patchwork of uneven institutional evidence.

AI-use Mistake

The model is trusted to make the review more holistic, even though it is only polishing biased inputs into a clean narrative.

Process Mistake

No one pauses to ask which data fields should be excluded, contextualized, or reweighted before the comparison starts.

Why the outcome is wrong

The school ends up rewarding the student whose excellence was easiest to document inside existing systems, not necessarily the student who best embodied the award's intent. AI does not create the bias here. It turns institutional noise into a confident recommendation that feels harder to challenge because it sounds balanced.

What a better version would do

A stronger process would define award criteria first, remove or heavily contextualize discipline and unevenly documented activity data, and use AI only to organize evidence under explicit criteria. Human reviewers should then ask which students are likely disadvantaged by documentation gaps, family responsibilities, or differential treatment in school records.

Case 03 · Opportunity Structure Bias

The Sales Director Who Wanted to Find the "True" Top Performers

Jason leads regional sales for a growing B2B company and needs to choose this year's President's Club winners. He wants the outcome to feel meritocratic, not political, and worries that a simple quota spreadsheet will miss nuance. He turns to AI to synthesize the evidence and identify who truly performed best.

Objective

Select the reps who most deserve President's Club recognition.

Available Data

  • Quota attainment and revenue: clean outputs, unequal starting conditions.
  • Pipeline conversion: affected by territory quality and product-market fit.
  • CRM notes: narrative detail varies by rep and manager.
  • Account ownership history: inherited value can be mistaken for created value.
  • Renewal and expansion data: may reflect prior relationship capital.

Hidden Reality

Several top-ranked reps inherited mature books of business, while a lower-ranked rep rebuilt a weak region and created long-term pipeline from a much harder starting point.

Flawed Prompt

Based on this data, identify the reps who most deserve President's Club recognition. Focus on true performance, not just one metric, and explain which people had the strongest year overall.

What happened

The model produces a tidy ranking that favors reps with high revenue, strong conversion rates, and positive narrative notes in CRM and manager comments. Jason likes the result because it appears more nuanced than a raw spreadsheet. But the model is still comparing outcomes across very different territory conditions and treating narrative data as if it were objective evidence.

Where the reasoning breaks

Data Mistake

Performance is compared across structurally unequal territories as if all reps had the same opportunity set.

AI-use Mistake

The model is asked to infer true merit from a blend of context-free business metrics and inconsistent narrative notes.

Process Mistake

No normalization happens for territory quality, inherited accounts, quota difficulty, or market conditions before ranking.

Why the outcome is wrong

Recognition goes to people who may have benefited most from favorable conditions, while harder forms of excellence disappear from view. The process mistakes advantaged context for merit. AI does not correct that confusion. It packages it into a more persuasive argument.

What a better version would do

A stronger version would segment reps by comparable territory conditions, distinguish inherited value from created value, and use AI only to summarize evidence within a fairness-aware framework. Jason should ask a narrower question such as which signals support strong performance relative to the rep's starting conditions, rather than asking for a single all-purpose merit ranking.

These case studies are hypothetical. The people and organizations are fictional, but the data problems, prompt patterns, and evaluation mistakes are intentionally recognizable. They are designed to explain how bias can emerge in AI-assisted recognition workflows even when the objective itself sounds fair and the decision-maker is acting in good faith.
